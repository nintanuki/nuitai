Mark Plays Nuitai

A blind playtest of the pygame demo by Mark, an invented professional playtester, thinking out loud. His notes follow the episode.

Mark This is the first session. I know the name, Nuitai, and that it is a 16-bit style RPG, and nothing else. There is a booklet in the box that I have not read, and I am going to leave it closed until the game makes me curious about something. What I want to find out in this hour: what kind of RPG this is trying to be, how the fights work, and whether the first stretch teaches its own rules or leans on the manual.

Mark Title screen. Sunset over open water, a single palm island on the horizon. The logo is painted half warm and half ocean blue, so the sea is probably the main character here. "New Game", "Continue" greyed out, "Options". Fresh cartridge. New Game it is.

Mark Opening cards, white text on black. "In a world covered by endless water, people live on scattered islands, venturing across the ocean at their own peril." Then sea monsters, storms that swallow ships, "miracles and oddities are daily routine." Then the hook: a small, verdant island far to the northeast, "a warmhearted young cook and a headstrong, devout priestess." So I already know my two leads by job before I see a face.

Mark "Kulāiwi Village. The last day of Summer." Last day of summer is a loaded date for a first scene. Something is ending.

Mark And here is the cook. Kailo. Red headscarf, three baskets of food by the door. "Stew for the wake tonight... cakes and breadfruit for the next island." A wake. So somebody died, and the opening is cooking for a funeral. Also a little popup, "Lore page added: 'Ulu". The game keeps a glossary. Good, I will want it with names like these.

Mark A girl runs up shouting his name. Melia. Boars in the taro patch, "Three of them... They're digging up everything!" Kailo: "That patch feeds three houses!" I like that line. It tells me the scale of this place in five words. Three houses depend on one patch.

Mark Then Hina cuts in. "Then stop shouting and do something about it." And she was coming over "for one of your taro cakes." Dry. My guess is Hina is the priestess from the intro, headstrong fits, but the game has not said so yet, so I will hold that.

Mark Melia wants to fight, gets told to watch the stew and the gulls, and pulls out a sling. "Fine, I'll get some practice with my sling." Kailo tells her to put it away. Small family scene, sibling energy. I'd bet that sling comes back.

Mark The boars. "SQUEEEE!" Three of them in the patch with the purple taro. A tutorial fight, I assume.

Mark "Preemptive strike!" and we cut to a battle screen. Hina is in the party, so she is fighting with me. She opens with "You do remember how to fight, right?" and gives me No or Yes, with No on top. I'll take No. First time player, I want the lesson, and I like that the game lets a returning player skip it in one press.

Mark "...Really? You lunkhead. Fine, I'll remind you." She is going to be the tutorial voice and she is going to be rude about it. That's a decent way to make a tutorial bearable.

Mark Interesting, the battle is on a beach. We were in a taro patch in the middle of the village a second ago. Small thing, but the backdrop doesn't match where the fight started.

Mark Turn order down the right side, faces stacked, "The top one goes now." And "What you pick decides how soon you go again." So it's a timeline system, like Final Fantasy X. Heavier actions push you down the list.

Mark Menu has Attack, Cook, Element, Item, with arrows on the sides. Attack opens a strike panel. Y is 1, X is 2, A is 3, and a yellow bar beside it. "Those yellow bars are stamina. Every stroke spends some." This is Chrono Cross. Light, medium, strong, out of a stamina pool. I know this system and I like it.

Mark Light hit for 6. A popup, "Lore page added: Lelua". Then "That blue under your HP is mana. Every hit you land builds it." Seven pips under each HP bar. "Each hit you land makes the next likelier." The light hit showed 88 percent, the next one 98, then 100. So the chain builds accuracy as it goes, also Chrono Cross.

Mark Strong for 42 and three pips of mana from one swing. Strong costs more turn time but pays in mana. The trade is clear, and the turn bar on the right shows the cost right away, which I like a lot. You can see your Kailo face slide down after a strong hit.

Mark Then Sauté. It lives under Element, and the header above it says "Lelua", with Kailo's two little icons, a flame and a wave. So Lelua is a group or a stance and Sauté is one move inside it. Sauté did 13. My strong hit did 42. That's a big gap for a move that costs the mana I just built. Maybe boars resist fire, maybe it does something besides damage. I will watch.

Mark Hina defends, and her own attack chain: 4, 12, 22, and her mana fills all seven. Another popup, "Māla Hula". Boar A took around 170 before it went down. These are not weak pigs.

Mark Now the blocking. "Each red bar is one of the pig's hits." "Press A as each bar crosses the yellow. Middle hurts least." "Dead center and you parry it. Not that you'll manage."

Mark The lesson box sits on top of the meter and the arrows wait for her to finish, which is considerate. Three blows. All three perfect. "Fine. That was good. Don't make it weird." Ha. I like her already.

What happened

- New Game. Opening cards about a water world and a small island to the northeast, then "Kulāiwi Village, The last day of Summer."

- Kailo is cooking for Old Pono's wake. Melia reports three boars in the taro patch. Hina joins for the fight.

- Fight 1, the tutorial (Kailo and Hina against Wild Boar A and B, preemptive strike): Hina teaches turn order, stamina strokes (Y light, X medium, A strong), mana, Sauté, Defend, Heal and blocking. Design notes

1. The battle system is a clear, confident take on Chrono Cross: light, medium and strong strokes from a stamina pool, hit chance climbing as the chain goes, mana built by landing hits and spent on elements. It teaches itself in the first fight. Praise, large.

2. The turn order bar on the right makes the cost of each choice visible. You see your face slide down after a strong hit. It also tells you when a Combo is possible, which turns it into a planning tool. Praise, large.

3. Blocking with a timed meter and a counterattack for a perfect parry keeps you busy on the enemy's turn. It's generous, though: three blows at even, predictable spacing every time. I never missed one. It will need variety later or it will go flat. Medium.

4. Stances do the work of an elemental system without a menu. The boar announces "shifts to nature stance", heals, and fire burns it and stops the healing. I worked it out from the messages before Hina explained it. Praise, large. Hina's hint, "Lanu's beasts always heal. Nature heals, but it burns.", comes after you've watched it heal twice. It belongs in the first fight. Small.

5. Sauté did 13 in the tutorial and looked useless. Its real job (burn and anti-heal) only shows up against a nature stance enemy. The tutorial should have the boar heal once so Sauté makes sense the first time you use it. Medium.

6. Cook is a good idea for a cook hero: a meal that feeds the whole party, with a minigame. But the first time you use it with an enemy next on the turn bar you lose all your mana and the meal, and the game gives no warning. The booklet's rule that a better stir costs more mana runs against what a player would guess. Medium.

7. The Combo, Pōhuehue, is the best moment of the hour. A rhythm part, then a spin and a crack at the top, for 138 damage. It costs all of both mana bars, so you lose the burn you might have needed. That's a real choice. Praise, large.

8. Hina's "the fallen get back up after a win" rule doesn't apply to her, the pond says "Everyone is rested" over a knocked out character, and the totem is the only revive. Each part is fine on its own. Together they left me walking a knocked out healer around for five minutes. Tell the player once, in the game, when Hina falls. Medium.

9. The save point is a prayer at a totem, where the Akua "remember it for us". It fits the world's idea that gods are made of Mana and memory. Praise, medium. The game should point it out once, ideally in the first village scene. Small.

10. Kailo feeds everyone after each win, which fits his character and keeps small fights from turning into HP management. Praise, medium.

11. The stew shopping list is a fetch quest, but one built on the hero's job, with a deadline (the wake) and a reason (the boar knocked over the pot). Noelani as a living quest log works. Praise, medium. Pork not dropping from boars, with no hint where it comes from, is the first place the quest feels opaque. Medium.

12. Words taught in context ("'A'ole", "'Ae", "Aloha", "Hale") and lore pages for names. The words land because the sentence tells you what they mean. Praise, small.

13. The dialect spellings (Haka and Shaka, Mauna and Mauga, Moanaku and Moanatu) are a nice idea, but only one of them is explained. Without that, they read like typos. Small.

14. Talking to someone whose conversation loops back to a question menu, and the menu taking the next z press, cost a lot of time with the Loremaster and Tutori. A clean "goodbye" at the end of each answer would fix it. Small.

15. The tutorial character named Tutori, with a light fourth wall joke ("The... menu?", "Button?"), is the right size: one kid, skippable with "This is boring". Praise, small.

16. Visible enemies on the map, with "!" when they notice you, plus ambushes and preemptive strikes. Good for a game with a deadline. Praise, small.

17. The battle background for the tutorial is a beach, not the taro patch. Small.

18. The PER and LUK swap between the victory page and the Equip page. Small, but it's the kind of thing that makes a player distrust the numbers.

19. The menu cursor remembering the last command can cost a turn. Small.

Story and characters

The story so far is small and warm, and something sad is sitting under it. It's the last day of summer on a tiny island. A beloved old man, Pono, has died and tonight is his wake. Kailo's father is gone, a villager says the island "takes people too young", and Kailo is raising a house of kids. The opening problem is boars in the taro and a ruined stew, and the game plays that straight and funny. But the world around it keeps hinting at bigger things: a foreign religion that took the sun from the local goddess a hundred years ago, sages in the Lost Woods who never forgave it, Callers in Makua who talk to the dead, and a rumor from the south that you can take Mana from a beaten champion.

- Kailo. The cook, nineteen by the booklet, warmhearted, hates waste ("That patch feeds three houses!", "a month of poi, just lying in the mud"). He's never left the islands. He has his dad's oar in a chest. He's likeable and a bit of a straight man to the women around him. He tells Keo the truth ("it's too dangerous") but promises to tell him everything. That's the kind of older brother he is.

- Hina. The priestess. Sharp, rude in a fond way ("You lunkhead", "Don't make it weird"), and the one who actually knows how to fight. She's leaving soon on a pilgrimage, a promotion. The Loremaster says "the young priestess" is from Kota Emas, where they say Shaka. If that's Hina, she isn't from here, and her two colors, flower and sun, are the two halves of the old split between Lanu and Haka. She joins by deciding to, not by being asked. I like her a lot.

- Melia. Kailo's little sister, confirmed ("big brother"). Wants to fight, has a sling, plays hide and seek, tells the Great Boar story like gospel. I think the sling comes back.

- Noelani. Lives in Kailo's house, cleans, cooks with him, holds his money and keeps the list. Kailo calls her "ma'am". The game hasn't said who she is to him.

- Chief Ulu. Kindly, proud of Hina, wants the stew at the wake.

- The Loremaster. The best writing in the hour. He holds both faiths without fuss: "I light a lamp for him, and I pour water for Lanu. Nobody ever told me I had to pick one." "A god is Mana that has learned to be someone."

- The Elder. Fished the reef with Pono for fifty years. Remembers Kailo's dad as "the loudest man on this beach."

- Tutori. The tutorial kid. "I'm just smart, I know things."

- Keo. A village boy who wants to see the Great Boar.

- Nene. Pono's widow, hungry, wants Kailo's poi, which Pono said was "the smoothest on the island". A villager says she hears Pono humming at night.

Themes forming: grief and the people left to carry a village; old faith and new faith living side by side, and the old resentment under that; staying home and leaving.

Review so far

Nuitai is trying to be a Chrono Cross for a Pacific island world, a small, warm story about a cook and a priestess on the last day of summer, with a battle system where magic is motion and the lore says the same thing the rules do. After an hour, most of that works. The fights are the strongest part: stamina chains, a visible turn bar, parries, stances you read from the enemy's own messages, and a two person Combo that hits like a truck and costs you something real. The writing has a light touch, Hina is a good partner, and the Loremaster has the best lines in the hour.

What doesn't work yet is the teaching around the edges. Cooking punishes you the first time with no warning. The healing pond says everyone is rested when someone is down. The save totem is never pointed out. The one ingredient you'd expect to get from the enemy you keep fighting never drops. None of those is serious alone, but they add up to a first hour where I had to open the booklet twice to get unstuck.

Would I recommend it today? To someone who loved Chrono Cross and is patient, yes. The battle system and the world are worth it, and the tone is its own. To someone new to this kind of game, not quite yet: the first hour needs a few more lines of in-game guidance before I'd hand it over without a warning.
